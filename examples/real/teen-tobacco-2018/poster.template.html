What the two surveys can tell us

Both are national surveys of U.S. students in school, asking about use on at least one of the past 30 days. The National Youth Tobacco Survey (NYTS) asks high school students (grades 9 to 12) about e-cigarettes. Monitoring the Future (MTF) asks 10th and 12th graders about vaping nicotine. Their levels are not expected to match; what matters is how each changed.

After the surveys' 2019 rounds came the vaping lung-injury outbreak (EVALI, August 2019 to February 2020, peaking in September 2019), the federal Tobacco 21 law (December 2019) and the FDA's flavor enforcement (January 2020); the 2020 rounds ran in early 2020, as the outbreak tailed off, until schools closed in mid-March. The NYTS saw high school e-cigarette use fall from 27.5% to 19.6%, with 95% intervals that do not overlap. MTF saw 12th graders' nicotine vaping go from 25.5% to 24.7% and 10th graders' from 19.9% to 19.3%; its authors report no significant change in past-30-day vaping, a fall in daily vaping, and a rise in how risky students thought vaping was. Both 2020 rounds were cut short by school closures: the NYTS had a 43.6% response rate, and MTF collected about a quarter of its usual sample. Neither survey can separate the outbreak from the policies.

The NYTS changed method three times: from paper to electronic in 2019, to an online survey that about half the students took away from school in 2021, and to an online survey in 2022. CDC ran no trend analysis across the 2019 change, said 2021 could not be compared with earlier years, and that comparing 2022 with earlier years is limited; the dotted steps cross those changes. MTF tested its 2019 change in a randomized half of schools, found no effect, and says its years compare directly.

Most outbreak cases were linked to THC vaping products with vitamin E acetate, outside both measures. The 2019 outbreak is often remembered as the “popcorn lung” scare, but popcorn lung (bronchiolitis obliterans) is linked to the flavoring diacetyl, a concern raised in 2015, not to the 2019 outbreak's main cause.
